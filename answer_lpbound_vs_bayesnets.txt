SHORT (2 paragraphs — best for an HN / Reddit / blog comment)

Thanks! The thing that clears it up: the ceiling isn't really estimating
anything — it never guesses the true row count, it just computes a number the
join provably can't exceed, from plain counting, no model or training. So it
isn't competing with a Bayesian network; it's more like a seatbelt around one.
A learned model is built to be accurate and usually is, on the queries it's
seen — but it can also be confidently wrong and talk the planner into a
terrible plan. The ceiling is the opposite: never especially accurate, but
never wrong in the dangerous direction. So the move isn't "pick one" — put your
smart estimate underneath the dumb-but-safe ceiling and keep both.

On subqueries and CTEs, it mostly depends on whether the planner turns them into
joins first — a lot of IN / EXISTS / derived-table stuff gets flattened into
joins anyway, and those are in scope. GROUP BY is a nice case: the result can't
have more rows than there are distinct groups, which is exactly what the HLL
sketch already knows, so you get a clean ceiling for free. Recursive CTEs it
won't touch, and for plain intermediate results the bound gets looser because I
only keep those stats on base tables. But it's still never below the truth, and
whenever it can't bound something cleanly it just steps back and lets the
engine's own estimate stand — so the worst these cases do is give you less help,
never a worse plan.

Thanks — and I think the framing that helps most is: the ceiling isn't really
trying to estimate anything. It never guesses the true row count. It just
computes a number the join provably can't exceed, from plain counting — no
model, no training. So it's not competing with a Bayesian network; it's more
like a seatbelt you'd put around one.

That's the honest comparison, really. A Bayesian net or graphical model is built
to be accurate — it learns the correlations in your data and usually predicts
well on the queries it's seen. The ceiling is the opposite personality: it's
never especially accurate, but it's never wrong in the dangerous direction
either. It can be loose, but it can't talk the planner into a catastrophic plan.
Where a learned model shines on familiar queries, the bound covers the
unfamiliar ones. So the natural move isn't "pick one" — it's put your smart
estimate underneath the dumb-but-safe ceiling and keep both.

On subqueries and CTEs: it mostly comes down to whether the planner turns them
into joins before samkhya looks. Modern optimizers flatten a lot of IN / EXISTS
/ derived-table stuff into joins already, and those land squarely in scope.
Anything with a GROUP BY is actually a nice case — the answer can't have more
rows than there are distinct groups, and that's exactly what the HLL sketch
already knows, so you get a clean ceiling for free. Recursive CTEs it won't
touch — the size depends on how the recursion unrolls, which no amount of static
counting can bound.

And the part I'd want to be upfront about: for a plain (non-recursive) CTE that
produces some intermediate result, the bound gets looser, because I only keep
those degree stats on your base tables, not on every intermediate. But it's
still never below the truth — and whenever it can't bound something cleanly, it
just backs off and lets the engine's own estimate stand. So the worst these
tricky cases do is give you less help, never a worse plan. That's really the
whole promise.
